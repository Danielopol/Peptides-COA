How to Read a Retatrutide COA

Retatrutide sits only ~82 Da from tirzepatide, which makes it the one compound in its family where a sloppy mass check can quietly pass the wrong molecule. Here's how to check the identity and purity sections of a retatrutide Certificate of Analysis.

October 13, 2026·9 min read·Research use only
Before anything else

Retatrutide is an investigational compound that has been studied in clinical trials. Material sold as a research chemical is not a pharmaceutical product, is not manufactured under GMP, and is not a substitute for any medicine. This article is about verifying documentation — reading a COA critically — and nothing here is medical advice or guidance for use in humans or animals.

New, heavily discussed compounds attract demand faster than reliable supply, and that gap is where weak paperwork thrives. A forged Certificate of Analysis costs a vendor nothing to produce, and most buyers can't tell a real one from a template edited in an image editor. The useful fact about retatrutide is that its molecular weight is fixed — so the single most important check on its COA is objective. The catch is that its closest neighbour is close.

The identity check: retatrutide's reference numbers

These are the values a retatrutide COA should be consistent with:

Two separate comparisons matter, and they catch different problems:

  1. The theoretical mass printed on the COA vs. the value above. If the document states a theoretical weight for "retatrutide" that isn't around 4,731 Da, it is internally wrong — a strong sign it was edited from another compound's template.
  2. The observed mass in the MS section vs. that theoretical value. This is what tells you the material tested was actually retatrutide.
How well-documented are these numbers?

Less thoroughly than for semaglutide or tirzepatide. Retatrutide did not have a PubChem entry when our reference table was compiled, so its formula and average mass come from published literature, and its monoisotopic mass was computed from the formula rather than taken from a database record. The figures are internally consistent and match the CAS-registered structure, but there are fewer independent places to cross-check them. Treat a deviation of a few daltons with more caution than you would for a compound with a public reference entry, and use the formula, not just the rounded mass, when something looks off.

Which number should match?

Monoisotopic and average mass differ by about 2.9 Da at this size. If the COA names a high-resolution technique (ESI, Q-TOF), compare against the monoisotopic figure of 4,728.47. If it just says "MW," it means average — 4,731.33. More on this in the molecular weight reference table.

Telling retatrutide apart from its neighbours

GLP-1 and related compounds are frequently confused, relabelled, or swapped. Most of them are hundreds of daltons from retatrutide, so mass spectrometry separates them easily. One is not:

Mazdutide is about 255 Da away and semaglutide about 618 Da — comfortably distinct. Tirzepatide is only about 82 Da away, the smallest gap in the family. That is still far outside instrument error, but it is small enough to disappear into a careless comparison: rounding to the nearest thousand, reading a mass off a chart axis, or comparing a monoisotopic observation to an average reference can all blur an 82 Da difference.

A worked example: the same sample in different charge states

Molecules this large rarely show a clean singly charged peak. They ionize into several charge states, and the spectrum shows m/z (mass divided by charge), not mass directly. For a protonated ion, m/z = (M + z × 1.007) ÷ z. Here is where each compound should appear:

Charge stateRetatrutide m/zTirzepatide m/zSeparation
[M+4H]⁴⁺≈ 1,183.8≈ 1,204.4≈ 20.5
[M+5H]⁵⁺≈ 947.3≈ 963.7≈ 16.4
[M+6H]⁶⁺≈ 789.6≈ 803.3≈ 13.7

A COA that lists a charge-state series and a deconvoluted mass makes this easy: the deconvoluted figure should land near 4,731, not 4,813. A COA that shows only a single unlabelled m/z value on a compound this size, with no stated charge, gives you nothing to anchor the comparison to — and is worth querying.

The substitution that matters

If a COA names retatrutide but the observed or deconvoluted mass sits near 4,813 Da, the material tested was tirzepatide, or the document was copied from a tirzepatide report with the name changed. Check the formula as well as the headline mass — C225H348N48O68 versus C221H342N46O68.

A near-miss worth knowing

An observed mass around 4,753 Da isn't a different compound — it's most likely a sodium adduct ([M+Na]⁺ adds about 22). Potassium adds about 38. Small satellite peaks at these offsets are normal and mildly reassuring that the spectrum is real data rather than a drawing.

Salt forms don't change the molecular weight

Retatrutide may ship as a TFA or acetate salt. The counterion adds weight to the powder in the vial, but it does not change the peptide's molecular weight — mass spectrometry still measures the peptide, so the figures above apply regardless of salt form.

What salt form does affect is how much actual peptide the vial contains by weight, a separate number entirely. See purity vs net peptide content.

The purity section

Retatrutide is a long, modified peptide, and compounds this size are harder to synthesise cleanly than short ones. Expect a purity figure supported by an actual chromatogram, not a bare number:

  • A purity percentage with decimal precision — 97.64%, not "98%". Round numbers across a vendor's whole catalog are a fabrication tell.
  • A stated specification — for example "Spec: ≥98%". A result without a spec is only half a result.
  • The HPLC chromatogram, with a sharp main peak, stable baseline, and visible integration lines.
  • Method parameters — column, mobile phase, flow rate, detection wavelength (214 nm or 220 nm).
  • Small impurity peaks. A chromatogram of a peptide this size with no minor peaks at all is less believable than one showing trace impurities at 0.1–1%.

For why an exact 100% is a signal in its own right, see why 100% purity is a red flag.

Red flags specific to retatrutide COAs

  • A theoretical or observed mass near 4,813. That is tirzepatide's mass. The single most important retatrutide-specific check.
  • Approval or "clinical grade" language. A compound that is still investigational has no approved pharmaceutical form for a vendor to match. Claims of "pharmaceutical grade," "GMP," or "clinical-trial material" without an accreditation or certificate number to verify are unregulated marketing language on a research-market document.
  • A single molecular weight for a blend. Products combining retatrutide with another compound are mixtures, and a mixture has no single molecular weight. A COA quoting one figure for a multi-component product is describing something that cannot exist.
  • The same chromatogram across different GLP-1 products. If a vendor's retatrutide and tirzepatide certificates show identical peak shapes, retention times, or impurity profiles, at least one is fabricated — these are different molecules.
  • Mass reported without a charge state on a molecule this large, with no explanation of how a small m/z value corresponds to a ~4,731 Da compound.
  • An old or missing analysis date on a fast-moving product line.
  • No mass spectrometry at all. HPLC alone establishes purity, not identity. For a compound with a close neighbour, a COA without MS data leaves the most important question unanswered.

These sit on top of the universal checks — lab identity, accreditation, batch traceability, dates. For the complete list see the field-by-field COA checklist and the forgery red flags guide.

What a retatrutide COA cannot tell you

  • It isn't a sterility or endotoxin test unless those tests are explicitly listed. Purity and identity say nothing about microbial contamination.
  • It describes the tested sample, not necessarily the vial you received — that link exists only through a matching batch number.
  • It says nothing about handling since testing. Peptides degrade with heat, moisture, and time.
  • It is not a quality equivalence to a pharmaceutical product. A clean COA on research-grade material does not make it one.

Frequently asked questions

What is the molecular weight of retatrutide?

Retatrutide has the molecular formula C221H342N46O68, an average mass of 4,731.33 Da, and a monoisotopic mass of 4,728.4718 Da. A COA quoting a single "MW" figure is normally referring to the average mass.

How can I tell if a retatrutide COA is fake?

Check that the stated theoretical mass is around 4,731 Da rather than tirzepatide's 4,813, that mass spectrometry data is present and matches it, that the lab is named with verifiable contact details and accreditation, and that the batch number matches your vial. Then look for tampering markers: mismatched fonts, pixelated fields, round purity numbers, or a chromatogram identical to one on another product's certificate.

How is retatrutide different from tirzepatide on a COA?

They are distinct molecules about 82 Da apart — retatrutide averages 4,731.33 Da and tirzepatide 4,813.45 Da — with different formulas (C221H342N46O68 versus C225H348N48O68). The gap is well outside instrument error but small enough to be missed by a careless comparison, so check the formula and any deconvoluted mass.

What purity should a retatrutide COA show?

Research-grade material is generally specified at ≥98%, reported with decimal precision and supported by a chromatogram showing the method parameters. A figure with no specification, no chromatogram, and no named method carries little weight.

Can a COA tell me whether research retatrutide is safe to use?

No. A COA reports identity and purity for a tested sample. It does not establish sterility, endotoxin levels, or suitability for any use in humans or animals, and research-grade material is not a pharmaceutical product.
